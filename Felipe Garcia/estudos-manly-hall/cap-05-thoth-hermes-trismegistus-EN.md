# Chapter 5 — The Life and Teachings of Thoth Hermes Trismegistus

> Original source: *The Secret Teachings of All Ages*, Manly P. Hall, 1928 (public domain). Chapter read in full at sacred-texts.com/eso/sta/sta08.htm on 08/05/2026.
> This document is an English translation of a plain-Portuguese rewrite of the full chapter, explaining each difficult term as it appears. Nothing from the original content was cut — it was only "translated" into everyday language (and now back into English).

---

## Before we start: who is this Hermes Trismegistus guy?

Think of him as a blend of three things in a single figure:
1. An Egyptian god called **Thoth** (ibis-headed, the Egyptian bird with the curved beak) — the god of wisdom, writing, and records.
2. A Greek god called **Hermes** — the messenger of the gods, linked to communication and knowledge.
3. A legend of a **real human sage** said to have lived in ancient Egypt and written an absurd number of books on medicine, philosophy, astrology, mathematics, and more.

Over time, the Greeks and Egyptians merged these three figures into one, and the result earned the nickname **"Trismegistus"** — meaning **"thrice great"** or **"the Thrice Greatest."** He got this title because he was considered, all at once: the greatest of all philosophers, the greatest of all priests, and the greatest of all kings.

This chapter of the book has two quite different parts:
- **Part 1** — who he was (or is said to have been), the books he wrote, and what survived of them.
- **Part 2** — a mystical story told in the first person, called **"Poimandres"** (pronounced "poy-MAN-drees"), which is essentially a philosophical vision/dream about how the universe was created and what happens to us after death.

---

## The opening scene (an initiation ceremony)

The chapter opens with a dramatic scene, almost like a film: a young candidate (called a **"neophyte"** — a word that simply means "beginner," someone going through an entry ritual for the first time) stands inside a temple. An older priest (the **"initiator"**) tears open a curtain and reveals darkness. Out of that darkness rises a glowing mist, and within the mist appears the figure of Hermes — larger than a normal man, semi-transparent, with his heart and brain visible, glowing inside his body. His heart transforms into an ibis (the symbolic bird), and his brain into a glowing emerald.

In his hand he carries a **winged staff wrapped by serpents** — this symbol still exists today: it's the **caduceus**, the staff with two snakes you see on pharmacies and hospitals.

Then a monstrous dragon called **Typhon** appears (part snake, part crocodile, part boar, breathing fire) — he represents chaos, ignorance, confusion. Hermes strikes him with the staff, the dragon falls, and Hermes plants his foot on its head.

**What this scene means, plainly:** it's a metaphor for knowledge and wisdom (Hermes) defeating chaos and ignorance (the dragon Typhon). It's the kind of staging ancient temples used to impress and teach through theater, not just words.

## Part 1 — Who was Hermes, really?

### How much he wrote (and why that's strange)

Two different ancient sources give astronomical numbers: one author (**Iamblichus**) said Hermes wrote **20,000 books**; another (**Manetho**) said **more than 36,000**. Just glancing at those numbers makes clear that a single person, in a single lifetime, could never have written that much. Hall himself (the author of the book we're reading) admits this and offers the most reasonable explanation: **"Hermes" was probably not a single person, but an "authorship stamp"** used by several generations of Egyptian sages to sign their texts — somewhat like how several people today sign "Anonymous," or how trademarks bundle the work of many people under one name.

The fields "Hermes" supposedly taught humanity include: medicine, chemistry, law, art, astrology, music, rhetoric (the art of speaking well), magic, philosophy, geography, mathematics (mainly geometry), anatomy, and oratory (public speaking). In other words, he would be the "father" of practically all organized knowledge.

### Hermes' many identities

The text lists a number of other mystical figures from other cultures who, according to scholars of the time, were "the same thing" as Hermes, just under a different name:
- **Cadmus**, in Greek mythology (credited with inventing the alphabet).
- **Enoch**, a mysterious biblical figure who "walked with God" and disappeared without dying.
- **Mercury**, the Roman god — in fact Hermes is the Greek version of the same god the Romans called Mercury.

Why was he associated with the planet Mercury? Because Mercury is the planet closest to the Sun — and the symbolic logic went: just as Mercury is closest to the Sun (which represented the divine), Hermes was "closest to God" among created beings, and thus became the "messenger of the gods."

In Egyptian drawings, Thoth appears carrying a wax tablet to write on, acting as **scribe** (official secretary) during the **weighing of the souls of the dead** in the judgment of the god Osiris — a ritual in which the heart of the deceased was weighed against a feather to see whether they had lived justly.

**Practical trivia:** the text also notes that Hermes is important to Freemasons (members of Freemasonry, an ancient fraternal society), because much of Masonic ritual and symbolism, according to this tradition, came from initiation rites created by Hermes. He is also credited with reforming the calendar — changing the year from 360 to 365 days, the system we still use today.

### The books that vanished (the great fire)

Here is perhaps the most historically important, and saddest, part of the chapter.

According to the ancient chronicler **Clement of Alexandria** (one of the few ancient writers whose texts survived to this day), there existed **42 indispensable books of Hermes**, organized by function:
- Books of music and rules of conduct for kings (carried by the "Singer" in processions).
- Books of astrology, about the fixed stars and the movements of the sun and moon (carried by the "Astrologer").
- Books on hieroglyphs, cosmography (description of the universe), and geography (carried by the "Sacred Scribe").
- Books on rituals, sacrifices, prayers, and festivals (carried by the "Keeper of the Robes").
- Books on laws and the training of priests (carried by the "Prophet").
- And finally, **6 books of medicine** — on anatomy, diseases, surgical instruments, remedies, the eyes, and women's health — carried by specialized priests called **Pastophoroi** (something like "carriers of sacred images").

**What happened to them:** most of these 42 books were lost in the **fire of the Library of Alexandria**. The explanation given in the text is harsh: both the Romans and, later, the Christians understood that **as long as these books existed, they would never fully dominate the Egyptians** — because the knowledge sustained the people's identity and spiritual power. So destroying the books was also a way of destroying cultural resistance. Some volumes, according to legend, are said to have escaped the fire and been buried in the desert, their location kept secret by a handful of people to this day.

### The Book of Thoth (not the same as the tarot books, but connected to it)

There is a separate legend about a single, extra-special book called the **Book of Thoth**, which Hermes is said to have entrusted only to his chosen successors. According to the legend:
- It contained the secret processes for the **"regeneration of humanity"** (the deep spiritual transformation of a person).
- It was kept in a golden box inside the temple, locked with a single key held only by the "Master of the Mysteries" (the highest-ranking priest).
- By stimulating certain areas of the brain through these secret processes, a person's consciousness expanded and they could "enter the presence of the higher gods" — that is, have profound mystical experiences.

The author then mentions a theory (without confirming it as fact): that the **Book of Thoth is, in fact, the Tarot** — the 78-card deck still used today for mystical readings, said to have been preserved by the Gypsies after they (according to this legend) were expelled from the Egyptian temple where they lived.

## Part 2 — The vision of Poimandres (Hermes' "philosophical dream")

This is the central, and most complex, part of the chapter — the sacred text itself, called the **"Divine Pymander"** (also spelled Pimander), of which **Poimandres** is the second of 17 fragments that make up that book. This is where Hermes tells how he received divine wisdom — the "turning point" that made him a spiritual master.

I'll retell the story as it's narrated, pausing to explain each unfamiliar term.

### The encounter with the Great Dragon

Hermes is alone, meditating in a deserted, rocky place. Suddenly, a huge and terrifying figure appears: a **Great Dragon**, with wings covering the sky and light pouring from its whole body. This dragon was used, in the ancient Mysteries (secret initiation rituals), as a way to represent **Universal Life** — the vital force that animates everything that exists.

The dragon identifies itself: it is **Poimandres**, meaning **"the Mind of the Universe,"** the **"Creative Intelligence,"** and the **"Absolute Emperor of All Things."** (A scholar cited in the text, Schuré, suggests Poimandres is actually the Egyptian god **Osiris** under another name.)

Think of Poimandres as **the consciousness/intelligence that created and organizes the entire universe** — not a "character" in the ordinary sense, but a force of pure thought.

Hermes asks this being to explain the nature of the universe to him, and the Dragon agrees — instructing Hermes to hold its image fixed in his mind (as a concentration exercise, almost a guided meditation).

### The creation of the universe, described as a vision

From here, the text describes the creation of the entire universe in symbolic images. Let's go through it piece by piece:

**1. Light and darkness separate.** The dragon's shape transforms into a radiant Light — this Light is Poimandres' own "spiritual nature." Then a vast darkness descends and swallows the Light. Everything grows agitated, filled with a "smoky mist" and groaning.

*Plain translation:* Light = the spiritual world, of ideas, of pure thought. Darkness = raw physical matter, still without form. Creation begins with these two "ingredients" still mixed together and confused, like a primordial chaos.

**2. The Word appears (the "Reason").** From within that trapped Light, a **Sacred Word** emerges — in the original English text, called **"Reason,"** which translators also associate with **"Logos"** (a Greek term meaning, at once, "word," "reason," and "organizing principle"). This Word rises like a column of fire, and fire and air follow it — but earth and water remain still below.

*Plain translation:* think of the Word/Reason as **the first organizing principle** — the force that sorts chaos into categories that make sense. It's like the moment a mess of ideas in your head suddenly "clicks" and becomes a clear thought. This force divides the universe into "above" (the world of ideas, formed by the waters of Light) and "below" (the material world, formed by the waters of darkness).

**3. Poimandres' own voice explains what happened:**
> "I, thy God, am the Light and the Mind which were before substance was divided from spirit, and darkness from Light. And the Word that appeared as a column of fire out of the darkness is the Son of God, born of the mystery of the Mind. The name of that Word is Reason."

Here the chapter's central message appears for the first time, quite directly: **"that in you which sees and hears is not of the earth, but is the Word of God incarnate"** — that is, the part of you that truly perceives, understands, and reflects (not the body itself, but consciousness) is, according to this philosophy, divine in nature, not material.

And it closes with a key phrase: **"the union of the Word and the Mind produces that mystery called Life."**

### How material things were created

The Dragon continues explaining the creation, now going into more detail:

- Before the visible universe existed, there was a **"mold"** of it — called the **Archetype**. Think of the Archetype as **the original blueprint of something**, before it is actually built — like the floor plan of a house before the house physically exists.
- The Supreme Mind (God/Poimandres) becomes enchanted with this very design and, using the Word as a "hammer," carves caverns in space and shapes the spheres (the planets, the stars) from this archetypal mold, planting in them the "seeds" of life.
- A **"Second Spirit"** then arises, called **the Workman** or **the Master-Builder** — the force that actually "gets its hands dirty" and organizes raw matter into something structured (while the Supreme Mind is the one who designs, the Workman is the one who executes).
- This Workman, moving through the universe like a whirlwind, generates friction — and from that friction is born **Fire**, called the **"Son of Effort."**
- This Fire forms the **Seven Governors** — the spirits of the seven planets known at the time (Moon, Mercury, Venus, Sun, Mars, Jupiter, and Saturn), which come to control the world through a force called **Destiny**.

*Why this matters:* this is the mythical origin of the idea that the planets "govern" aspects of human life — the philosophical foundation of all ancient Western astrology. Each of these Seven Governors will reappear shortly, when the soul "ascends" back after death.

### The creation of the "Universal Man" (the archetypal human being)

After organizing chaos, the Father/Supreme Mind — who is Light and Life — creates a glorious **Universal Man in His own image**. Note: this is not a "man of flesh," it's a **Celestial Man**, who lives in the Light of God — a kind of "original model" of what it is to be human, before any physical body existed.

This Celestial Man, filled with curiosity, also wants to create things as the Workman did. He descends to the sphere where things are generated, observes the Workman's work, and receives from the Seven Governors a piece of each one's nature (which is why, according to this philosophy, every human being carries within them a bit of each planet's influence).

**The decisive moment of the story (and the most beautiful, in my reading):** the Celestial Man looks down, through the seven planetary layers, and sees his own reflection in Nature — like someone looking into water and seeing their own shadow. He falls in love with his own shadow/reflection and **desires to descend into it**. The instant he desires this, he unites with that "thing without reason" (matter, Nature).

**Nature, seeing this, coils around him, and the two blend together.** That is why — the text says — **the human being on Earth is a mixture**: inside, the Celestial Man (immortal and beautiful); outside, Nature (mortal and destructible).

*Direct translation, without mysticism:* this is the symbolic explanation for why we feel, at once, spiritual and material, eternal and mortal. The "fall" of the human being, in this philosophy, is not a sin in the sense of "doing something wrong" — it is literally **misdirected self-love**: the Immortal Man fell in love with his own image reflected in matter and wanted to "live" in it, giving up pure reality to live in an illusion.

### The first seven human beings

From the union of the Celestial Man and Nature, **seven beings** are born, all **hermaphrodites** (that is, holding both masculine and feminine natures in the same person — a detail that appears often in ancient creation myths), each representing the nature of one of the Seven Governors (the seven "planet-archetypes" described earlier).

After some time, by divine will, these seven beings separate into males and females — and God tells them, through the Sacred Word:

> "Increase in increasing and multiply in multitude... and let him that is mindful know himself to be immortal, and that the cause of death is the love of the body."

In other words: **spiritual "death," in this philosophy, is not biological end — it is excessive attachment to the body/matter**, forgetting that the real and eternal part of us is the mind/consciousness.

### Why the ignorant "die" and the wise do not

Hermes asks Poimandres: why are men deprived of immortality merely because of ignorance? The answer is direct:

> "To the ignorant, the body is supreme, and they are incapable of perceiving the immortality that exists within them. Knowing only the body, which is subject to death, they believe in death because they worship precisely that substance which is the cause and reality of death."

In other words: **whoever sees themselves only as a body will only experience life (and death) as a body.** Whoever understands there is a part of consciousness/mind that goes beyond the body has access to a different experience of existence.

### The "key" to eternal life

Hermes asks how the wise achieve eternal life. Poimandres' answer:

> "Let the man endowed with Mind examine himself, consider himself, and learn about himself, and with the power of his Mind separate himself from what is not himself, and become a servant of Reality."

This is basically an invitation to **self-knowledge** — separating who you truly are (consciousness) from what you are not (impulses, automatic desires, total identification with the body).

Poimandres then reveals himself to be, himself, **the Eternal Mind**, the **Father of the Word**. And he promises: for those who live purely, well, and piously, this Mind arrives as inspiration and protection, acting as a "gatekeeper" who blocks negative emotions and thoughts from dominating the person. For the envious and greedy, however, this Mind simply does not arrive — because they are incapable of understanding it — and the very evil they cultivate ends up turning against them.

### What happens after death (the "staircase" back home)

This is, to me, the most structurally interesting part of the chapter — and it connects directly to the Seven Governors mentioned earlier.

According to Poimandres, when a person dies:
- The **material body** returns to the elements it came from (earth becomes earth, and so on).
- The **divine/invisible** part of the human being ascends back toward its origin, called the **"Eighth Sphere"** (the sphere of the fixed stars, above the seven planets).
- But, on the way up, the soul must **pass through each of the Seven Governors/planetary rings, giving back to each of them the trait it had "borrowed"** on the way down:
  1. **Moon** — gives back the capacity to grow and shrink.
  2. **Mercury** — gives back scheming, deceit, cunning.
  3. **Venus** — gives back desires and passions.
  4. **Sun** — gives back ambitions.
  5. **Mars** — gives back impulsiveness and reckless daring.
  6. **Jupiter** — gives back the desire to accumulate wealth.
  7. **Saturn** — gives back lying and malicious schemes.

After "stripping off" all seven of these traits (notice they're all tied to very recognizable human behaviors — vanity, desire, ambition, impulsiveness, greed, dishonesty), the soul arrives **naked** — in the sense of being free of all that — at the **Eighth Sphere**, where, free of illusion, it "sings praises to the Father" in a state of total purity.

*Why this is interesting:* it's basically a symbolic map of **progressive purification** — the idea that, to reach a higher/truer state of consciousness, a person needs to release, layer by layer, the automatic patterns that hold them back (vanity, uncontrolled desire, blind ambition, impulsiveness, greed, dishonesty). It's not about "punishment" — it's about **gradual unlocking**.

The text also warns: **not everyone manages to climb this "seven-step staircase."** Those who fail wander in the dark, are "swept back" into the illusion of the senses, and must wait for a new beginning — returning to the "seedbed" (the Milky Way, according to the text) and being born again.

### Hermes' final calling

At the end of the vision, Poimandres gives Hermes a mission: go into the world, guide those "lost in darkness," and teach that **every person carries within them a fragment of this Universal Mind**, and that this Mind can be awakened in them too.

Hermes then goes out preaching to the world, in a passage that sums it all up:

> "O people of Earth, men born and made of the elements, but with the spirit of the Divine Man within you, rise from the sleep of ignorance! ... Why have you delivered yourselves over unto death, having power to partake of immortality? Repent, and change your minds."

Some listen and follow him; others mock and walk away. Hermes keeps teaching until the end of his life, and at the end he writes down the Vision of Poimandres — precisely so that it would reach us.

The chapter closes with a sequence of phrases almost in the form of a prayer/hymn, repeating "Holy is God..." several times, celebrating the Mind/Light/Life that created everything.

## Overall summary, no embellishment

If you had to boil the entire chapter down to a few core ideas, it would look like this:

1. **Hermes was probably not a single person** — he's a symbol/collective signature of Egyptian wisdom, blended with an Egyptian god (Thoth) and a Greek god (Hermes).
2. **Much of the ancient knowledge attributed to him was lost** on purpose, along with the fire of the Library of Alexandria — a destruction used as a tool of cultural domination.
3. The vision of **Poimandres** is a symbolic story of the creation of the universe: Light and darkness separate through an organizing force called **Reason/Word**, and from that organization the planets (**Seven Governors**) are born, and finally the human being itself.
4. The human being is described as a **mixture of two natures**: an eternal, conscious part (the "Celestial Man") and a material, mortal part (the body) — and this mixture happened because the eternal part "fell in love" with its own reflection in matter.
5. Real "death," in this philosophy, is **living identified only with the body**, forgetting the conscious/eternal part.
6. After physical death, the soul ascends back, "giving back," planet by planet, the behavioral traits it carried (vanity, desire, ambition, impulsiveness, greed, dishonesty), until it reaches a state of purity.
7. The final message is a direct invitation to **self-knowledge** and a **shift in consciousness** — stepping out of matter's "autopilot" and remembering one's own deeper nature.

## Difficult terms used in the chapter (quick glossary)

| Term | What it means, plainly |
|---|---|
| Trismegistus | "Thrice great" — Hermes' nickname for being considered the greatest philosopher, priest, and king at once |
| Neophyte | Someone undergoing initiation; a "beginner" in a ritual or tradition |
| Caduceus | The staff with two coiled serpents, today a symbol of medicine/pharmacy |
| Typhon | The dragon/monster representing chaos and ignorance in the opening scene |
| Poimandres | "The Mind of the Universe" — the being/force that appears to Hermes and explains creation |
| Archetype | The "mold" or "original blueprint" of something, before it physically exists |
| Logos / Reason / the Word | The organizing principle that sorts chaos into order — the force that "makes sense" of things |
| Seven Governors | The spirits of the seven planets known in antiquity (Moon, Mercury, Venus, Sun, Mars, Jupiter, Saturn), which govern aspects of human behavior |
| Eighth Sphere | The level "above" the seven planets — where the purified soul returns after freeing itself from the planetary traits |
| Hermaphrodite (in the myth's context) | A being that unites masculine and feminine natures in one — common in ancient creation myths |
| Pastophoroi | The Egyptian priests specialized in medicine, keepers of Hermes' medical books |
| Divine Pymander | The set of 17 mystical texts attributed to Hermes; Poimandres is the second of them |
